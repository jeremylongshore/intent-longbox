## 2. Decision A — Three principals, four roles, and no fourth of either

### 2.1 The rule

> **This system has exactly three authenticable principals: the `app_user` (a person), the `device` (an enrolled phone or kiosk), and — already shipped — the database role (E02-D06). A shop is a scope, never a principal. A role is one of 034 §2.6's four, and a session never invents a fifth.**

034 §2.5 and §2.8 already ratified the *subjects*: `app_user` (a person with a login, no `shop_id`, because a person can hold roles in more than one scope) and `device` / `device_credential` / `device_credential_revocation` (*"the phone as an auth principal"*). This record adds the **mechanism** those sections deliberately excluded and changes neither subject.

### 2.2 The four roles are 034's, and the bead's three-role framing is corrected

The bead's directives name the roles as *owner / staff / longbox-support break-glass*. **034 §2.6 ratified four, as a closed CHECK enum on `membership.role`:** `owner`, `manager`, `operator`, `support_break_glass`. This record uses those four unchanged and does not re-argue the enum-versus-table decision.

The three-role reading is natural because **`manager` is absent from the representative shop** — 034 §2.6 sources it to *"the 19% with a second storefront"*. It is still wrong, and the reason is a specific hazard rather than pedantry: a role that exists in the schema and not in the pilot is exactly the role an implementation drops silently and a second shop discovers by being unable to express its own staffing. **`manager` is implemented and untested-by-a-real-shop at G2, and §11 I13 asserts it is implemented rather than allowing the pilot's shape to prune it.**

**What this record adds to §2.6 and nothing more:** the mapping from role to *authentication requirement*, which is §4's table. It adds no permission, no matrix and no check — **that is E03-B03's**, and §12.3 says so.

### 2.3 A shop is a scope, and the consequence is the whole of §6

`membership` (034 §2.7) is the join, and it is append-only with a separate `membership_revocation` — *"a role change is two rows, not one"*. So **the set of shops a caller may act in is a query over facts, not a column on a session**, and it can change while a session is live. §3.4 is where that lands: a membership write **rotates** every live session of the affected person, so a revoked employee's phone stops working at the next request rather than at the next expiry.

### 2.4 What is deliberately NOT a principal

| Not a principal | What it is instead | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| **A shop** | a scope, resolved from the session's memberships (§6.1) | making a shop a principal is what a shared shop password *is*, and the bead's own note refuses it: **"No global pilot password."** |
| **A browser tab** | nothing; a session is bound to a device and a person, never to a tab | a per-tab identity would make *"who confirmed this book"* a function of a window the operator closed |
| **A batch or a bin** | work context (034 §2.10, §2.11), never authority | 022 P2: an override must be cheap, so authority is never re-established mid-batch |
| **An IP address** | not identity and not a rate key (042 §8.1) | a counter's Wi-Fi is one IP for a whole shop and two shops behind one carrier are one IP |

## 3. Decision B — Two sessions, not one

### 3.1 The rule

> **A device session and an operator session are separate records with separate lifetimes. The device session authenticates the app instance and pins the shop and the location; the operator session authenticates the person and pins the attribution. Both are server-side records; the browser holds an opaque token and nothing else. Neither has a status column: liveness is derived.**

This is 034 §2.8's construction made concrete, and 034 already argued why one principal cannot do the job: 022 P3 requires that an employee's self-view *"never [renders] on a shared or kiosk device without fresh authentication"*, while 033 §1 A13 (`033:41`) requires a shared counter phone to pass between two people — *"Next item — no re-login, no re-picking the box"* — between books. **Those two requirements are contradictory with one principal and trivially compatible with two.**

### 3.2 Server-side record, not a signed self-contained cookie

Three constructions were available.

**(a) A signed, self-contained cookie** (a JWT or a signed session object) with no server record. *Rejected*, and it is the honest alternative: it needs no table, no read on the hot path and no migration. What it cannot do is **stop**. Revocation of a self-contained token requires a denylist consulted on every request — which is a session table with a worse name, an unbounded growth problem and a failure mode where forgetting to consult it is invisible. And revocation is not an edge case here: it is §2.3's membership change, §3.4's operator switch, §7's lost-phone case and §8's recovery, all of which must take effect **now** and not at the next expiry.

**(b) Sticky server-side session state on the connection or in process memory.** *Rejected on evidence already in this repository.* `src/services/rateLimit.ts:80-139` holds two `Map`s, and 046 E25 records the three consequences: the buckets are per **process**, so two processes give one shop two budgets; a restart empties them; and nothing survives a deploy. **A rate bucket that resets on deploy is a nuisance; a session store that resets on deploy signs every operator out mid-batch, and one that does not reset because it is sticky on a pooled connection is 034 §3.2's `SET LOCAL` hazard wearing different clothes.**

**(c) An append-only session record in Postgres, an opaque token in the cookie.** **Adopted.** The token is 256 bits from a CSPRNG; the database stores `sha256(token)` and never the token. Every property this record needs — revocation, rotation, reuse detection, the audit trail T35(c) requires — is a *fact about a session*, and this system already has one correct place to put a fact.

**Why SHA-256 and not a slow KDF for the session token, stated because the reflex is the other way.** A password KDF (argon2id, bcrypt) exists to make a **low-entropy, human-chosen, long-lived** secret expensive to guess offline. A session token is a **256-bit, machine-generated, short-lived** secret: there is no dictionary, offline guessing is not a threat model, and a per-request KDF is a denial-of-service surface the attacker controls the rate of. **The PIN and the password get argon2id (§3.5, §4.2); the session token gets a fast hash, and the difference is the entropy of the input, not the sensitivity of the output.**

### 3.3 No status column, and idle expiry without a mutable `last_seen_at`

> **`app_session` is append-only. There is no `status`, no `active` boolean and no `last_seen_at`. A session is live iff a row exists, no revocation names it, no successor supersedes it, and now is before both its absolute and its idle expiry.**

040 A8 retired `scan_session.status`; 042 I5/§3.3 generalised the leak it caused; 047 §5.1 refused to reintroduce one on the LCID registry. Reintroducing one here would be the worst of the three, because a session's state is the one thing in the system that two rows disagreeing about is a **security** outcome rather than a reporting one.

The hard part is idle expiry, which naively wants a column that moves on every request. **It is solved by rotation rather than by mutation:**

> **A session is re-issued rather than touched. Each request whose session is older than the rotation period issues a NEW `app_session` row, chained by `rotated_from`, sets the new cookie, and leaves the old row in place as spent. `idle_expires_at` is computed at issuance from the rotation period; a session that is not used within it is never re-issued and simply expires.**

Four things fall out, and they are the argument:

1. **Idle and absolute expiry are both properties of immutable rows.** `issued_at`, `absolute_expires_at` and `idle_expires_at` are written once and never corrected.
2. **Rotation is free where it is mandatory anyway** — on privilege change (§3.4), on operator switch (§3.5) and on MFA completion (§4.3) — so the mechanism is not built twice.
3. **Token reuse becomes detectable — later, and only sometimes (R4, struck as an overclaim at ratification).** Presenting a token whose row already has a successor, or a revocation, is **evidence that the cookie was copied**, because the legitimate client received the successor. The response is to refuse *and* revoke the operator chain (K2, below), which turns a stolen cookie into a signed-out attacker and a signed-out operator, whose re-authentication is one PIN.
   > **What v1.0.0 claimed and what is true.** v1.0.0 called this *"the single strongest property in §3"*. **It is not, and the correction is the security lens's D1.** The detector fires **only when the legitimate client subsequently rotates**. An attacker who copies a cookie and uses it *before* the victim's next rotation is indistinguishable from the victim; an attacker who copies a cookie from a phone that is then put down for the night triggers nothing until morning. **The load-bearing control against a copied cookie is idle expiry** — a bounded window in which a stolen token is worth anything at all — and the reuse detector is a **second, later signal that the theft happened**, not a barrier that stops it. It is still worth having, and it is still unavailable to construction (a). It is not a defence, and no artifact may describe it as one.
4. **The write volume is bounded by the rotation period, not by the request rate.** A scan session is a burst of eight or nine requests over a couple of minutes (E2's route list); a rotation period measured in tens of minutes makes rotations rare relative to requests. **The period is a PROVISIONAL floor with its derivation stated and no measurement behind it** (042 A3): it is short enough that a stolen cookie's window is bounded by the next legitimate request, and long enough that the row count per operator-day is smaller than the row count per operator-book. **It is set by E03-B02's build from the first measured rotation-per-request ratio on a real batch, and until then it is a configured value that no artifact quotes as a security property.**

`app_session_revocation` is the ending, with `UNIQUE (session_id)` — 034 §2.7's grant/release idiom, chosen there for exactly this reason: *"A revocation says 'that grant ended'; the earlier row was never wrong, and there is no replacement."* `rotated_from` carries `UNIQUE` for the same reason 041 R2 and 047 §6.1 do: **a session is superseded at most once, so the chain is a chain and never a fork** — and a second successor is precisely what a token-theft race looks like, so the constraint is a detector.

#### `chain_id`: liveness in constant time, not in walk depth (R2)

> **Every `app_session` row carries a `chain_id uuid NOT NULL`, generated at the chain's first issuance and copied unchanged into every successor. A revocation names a `chain_id`, never a walk. Liveness is one indexed row read plus two indexed `NOT EXISTS` — constant in rotation depth.**

The derivation in v1.0.0 was correct and unbounded: *"no revocation names it, no successor supersedes it"* is a walk back up `rotated_from` on every request, and a phone worked all day is a chain hundreds of rows long. **A security check whose cost grows with how long the shift has been is a security check that gets cached, and a cache of liveness is the status column arriving through the side door** — which is the Kleppmann-lens observation this amendment exists to pre-empt and the Hickey substitution would have made first.

`chain_id` is not a status and not a cache. It is the **identity of the chain**, written once per chain and never updated, in exactly the sense `scan_session.id` is the identity of a session (locked decision 4). The three predicates:

1. `SELECT … FROM app_session WHERE token_hash = $1` — one index hit.
2. `NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM app_session_revocation WHERE chain_id = $2)` — one index hit, and this is what makes revocation **O(1) instead of O(chain)**: revoking a chain is one row, not one row per session ever issued in it.
3. `NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM app_session WHERE rotated_from = $3)` — one index hit on the `UNIQUE (rotated_from)` index that already has to exist.

Plus the two timestamp comparisons on the row itself. **`app_session_revocation` therefore keys on `chain_id`, not `session_id`** — the `UNIQUE` moves with it, and its meaning sharpens: *this chain ended*, which is the fact anybody ever wanted to record. Revoking one row in the middle of a live chain was never a thing this system needed to express.

#### The read, the write, and the lock order (R3, K1, K2, K3)

> **The session read is `SELECT … FOR NO KEY UPDATE`, taken INSIDE the request transaction `runIdempotent` already opens (`src/services/idempotency.ts:247`, `src/db.ts:105`). The rotation write lives in that same transaction, so a request that rolls back issues no successor.**

Three consequences, each of which was a gap in v1.0.0.

**(a) The lock, and why `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` (R3).** A bare `SELECT` cannot support §3.4's *"in the same transaction"* claim, because two transactions can both read a live session and both act on it. `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` is the weakest lock that serialises writers against this row while still permitting concurrent readers that take no conflicting lock — the same choice 041 §4.2 makes for the `scan_session` anchor, and the reason is the same: a plain `FOR UPDATE` would block foreign-key checks referencing the row for no benefit. **The lock is on the row the whole security model reads, which is the cost Q2 asks about and §13.0's ruling on Q2 states plainly.**

**(b) It joins 042 §5.3(b)'s declared lock order at a stated position (K1).** 042 §5.3(b) fixes one order in every mutating handler, *"with no exceptions"*: the `request_idempotency` INSERT is taken **before** the anchor `SELECT … FOR UPDATE`. This record inserts the session lock **between** them:

> **`request_idempotency` INSERT → `app_session` row (`FOR NO KEY UPDATE`) → `scan_session` anchor (`FOR UPDATE`).**

The position is argued, not assumed. Identity-of-the-request stays first for 042's own reason — a replay must be recognised before it locks any domain state. The session lock comes second because **authentication is a precondition of touching the subject at all**: a request that is going to be refused for a dead session must not first take a lock on a live session's anchor. And the anchor stays last, unchanged. **042 §5.3(b)'s lint (I22) is extended to police three positions rather than two**, which is a one-line change to a rule that already exists and the reason it exists — a handler written six months from now by someone who has not read this section.

**(c) The rotation race, and the grace window (K2).** Two requests from the same client can both read the same live session and both attempt to rotate it. `UNIQUE (rotated_from)` makes exactly one of them win; the question v1.0.0 left open is what the loser does. If the loser treats its own token as reused, **the security control manufactures the outage it exists to prevent** — a phone that double-taps signs itself out, which is the Kleppmann lens's finding 2 stated in its own words.

> **The loser re-reads, finds the winner's successor, and accepts it — only if the successor is bound to the same `device_id` and the same `app_user_id`, and only within a stated grace window measured from the successor's `issued_at`. Outside that window, or on any binding mismatch, it is reuse and is treated as reuse.**

The grace window is a **PROVISIONAL floor** (042 A3, §0): short enough that a stolen cookie cannot ride a legitimate rotation, long enough to cover a double-tap and one network retry. It is not a measurement and no artifact quotes it.

> **And reuse revokes the OPERATOR chain, never the device chain.** A false positive that signs out an operator costs one PIN (§3.5). A false positive that signs out a **device** costs an owner in a privileged session, an enrollment code and a walk to the back room — during trading hours, on the shared counter phone, for a race the operator caused by tapping twice. **The blast radius of the detector is deliberately smaller than the blast radius of a lost phone**, and the two are separated because §3.1 made them separate principals.

### 3.4 Rotation is mandatory on privilege change

> **Any write to `membership` or `membership_revocation` naming an `app_user` rotates every live session that person holds, in the same transaction.**

Not expires — **rotates**, so the person keeps working and the new session carries the new scope. The transaction boundary is the point: a role change that commits while a session on the old scope is mid-request must not leave a window in which the old scope is still resolvable, and `withTransaction` (`src/db.ts:105`) is already the envelope every write runs inside. A **revocation** of the last membership at a shop rotates to a session with no membership there, which §6.1 resolves as *no shops*, which the client renders as signed out.

**"In the same transaction" is not sufficient on its own, and the missing half is a lock (K3, answering Q6).** Q6 asked whether the rule holds under concurrency. It does not, as drafted. A membership write and an in-flight request on the affected session run in **different transactions on different connections**; the membership transaction can rotate a session the request transaction read a microsecond earlier, and the request then completes on the old scope with nothing having gone wrong anywhere. *"Same transaction"* orders the membership write against the rotation; it orders **neither against the request**.

> **The membership write takes `SELECT … FOR NO KEY UPDATE` over every live session of the affected `app_user` — across every shop, and both chains — before it inserts the `membership` or `membership_revocation` row. Sessions are locked in `chain_id` order to make a deadlock between two concurrent membership writes unconstructible.**

**This is a lock on the hot path of every request, and the answer to Q6 is therefore yes.** §3.3(a)'s `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` on the session row is what the membership write contends with; without it there is nothing to contend with and the rule is prose. The cost is stated rather than buried: a membership write blocks behind every in-flight request of that person, and every request of that person blocks behind a membership write. **Both are correct outcomes and both are rare** — a membership write is a hiring, a firing or a promotion, and the alternative is a fired employee's phone working until its next rotation. §12.2 records it as one of the things that gets worse.

**Ordering by `chain_id` is the same move 042 §5.3(b) makes**: two administrators changing two roles that share a person would otherwise be free to take the same two session rows in opposite orders, and Postgres would resolve it with a `40P01` at the counter. A fixed order makes it unconstructible rather than rare.

### 3.5 The shared counter phone: a device session plus a PIN, and staff never hold a password

This is the section the bead is really about, and the honest statement of the problem is 033 §1 A13's: one phone on a counter, passed between two people, with no re-login between books — against 019 T35 and 022 P3, which require that what each person did is attributable and that nobody may render a per-operator surface.

> **The device session is long-lived and possession-bound. An operator session is a short session on top of it, established by the person tapping their name and entering a personal PIN. Switching operator is one tap and a PIN — never an email and a password. A PIN is verified only on a device that already holds a live device session, and is useless anywhere else.**

**Why a low-entropy secret is acceptable here and nowhere else.** A PIN is indefensible as a primary web credential and entirely defensible as a **knowledge factor on a possession-bound channel**: the attacker must first hold an enrolled device with a live device session, which is a physical object behind a shop counter. The three controls that make it hold are stated as obligations rather than assumed: the PIN is verified with argon2id **plus a process-environment pepper** (§9.2), the lockout is per `(device_id, app_user_id)` and is a **fact** rather than an in-memory counter (§9.1), and a PIN never authenticates anything but an operator session — it can neither sign in on another device nor reach a privileged surface (§4.1).

**The PIN's shape, fixed rather than ranged (R6).**

> **Six digits. Not four, and not a range.** A range in a decision record is a range an implementation resolves downward, and the difference between four digits and six is two orders of magnitude of guessing budget against a lockout whose whole design (§9.1, R5) is that it **degrades to delay rather than closing a door** — so the guessing budget is never zero and the digit count is doing real work. **A trivial-PIN denylist is enforced at set time**: sequences (`123456`, `654321`), repeats (`111111`), the shop's own postcode or phone digits, and the standard leaked-PIN head. A denylist is refused at *set* time and never at *verify* time, because a verify-time check leaks which PINs are impossible.
>
> **A PIN is never compared across scopes and is never a recovery answer** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). The verification function takes exactly one `(device_id, app_user_id)` pair and refuses any other — it does not "try the person's other PINs", does not fall back to another device's row, and a PIN is never accepted as an identity proof in the §8 recovery path. A credential that is worthless off one device stops being worthless the moment one code path compares it somewhere else.
>
> **A membership revocation retires that person's `operator_pin` rows at that scope** (RECOMMENDED, adopted), in the same transaction as §3.4's rotation. Otherwise a fired employee's PIN sits live on the counter phone against a membership that no longer exists, and the only thing standing between it and an operator session is a membership check somebody has to remember to write.

**The MECHANISM is an append-only fact, ruled at v1.4.0 by E03-D07** (`operator_pin_retirement`, `migrations/024`), not the nullable `retired_at` column `migrations/020` first gave it, and PIN liveness is a predicate over both. The decisive reason is local to this row: **`operator_pin` is §9.1's LOCKOUT ANCHOR**, taken `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` by every PIN verification, so an `UPDATE` retirement writes the security-critical row from a path that is not a PIN verification — beside a sequence whose correctness argument assumes the row's only writer is a PIN change. It also matches every other ending in this schema (`membership_revocation`, `device_credential_revocation`, `app_session_revocation`, each with its own UNIQUE), and it keeps the **when** and the **why** that an `UPDATE` destroys and that T35(c) asks for. `retired_at` is left in place and still read — 000-docs/044 §2 makes dropping it a contract step.

**The insider residual, stated verbatim rather than mitigated (R7).**

> **A coworker who watches a PIN being entered on a shared counter phone can, from that moment, act as that person on that phone. The system will record the act as theirs and will be unable to distinguish it from the real thing. No control in this record prevents that, and none is proposed: the possession factor is a phone both people are standing at, and the knowledge factor is six digits typed in front of a colleague.**

**And the rule that follows from it, which binds every artifact this project produces:**

> **RULE. No artifact, dissent, 021 registered claim, pilot-charter clause, partner-facing sentence or support answer may describe operator attribution in this system as non-repudiable, as tamper-proof, or as proof of who performed an act.** *(EXTENDED at v1.6.0 by E03-D11 to the PRIVILEGED principal, in one clause, because the third session kind is the obvious place somebody would assume otherwise: a password and a TOTP code prove possession of two secrets and not the presence of a person, and a shoulder-surfed code beside a written-down password is the same class of fact as a watched PIN. **The rule covers every principal this system has, and a stronger factor does not buy a stronger claim.**)* It is an **attribution of record** — the best available account of who was signed in — and it is exactly as strong as the PIN, which is exactly as strong as who was watching. 019 T35(c)'s reconciliation, 022 P1's blame clause and any accountability language in 039 are all downstream of this sentence. **021 owns the enforcement**: a C-row that says or implies otherwise is refused at the T26 pre-send check.

**Cross-chain liveness, as a formula rather than a sentence (K4).** An operator session sits on top of a device session, and the device session rotates on its own schedule. v1.0.0 said the operator session "names its `parent_session_id`", which is ambiguous the moment the parent rotates: the named row is spent, and a naive liveness check on it kills every operator session on the phone every rotation period.

> **`live(op) := live(op.row) ∧ live(current-successor-closure of op.parent_session_id)`**
>
> where `live(row)` is §3.3's three predicates plus the two timestamps, and the successor closure is resolved **by `chain_id`, in one indexed read** — which is the second reason `chain_id` exists (R2). A parent that has rotated is still live; a parent whose **chain** is revoked or expired is not, and every operator session on it dies at the next request with no sweep.

> **`shop_id` and `location_id` are denormalized onto the operator session row at issuance**, copied from the device session's enrollment. They are immutable facts about an issuance, not a cache of a mutable elsewhere — the device's enrollment cannot change under a live session, because re-enrolling a device is a new `device_credential` and §7.3's revocation kills the chain. **So a parent rotation is harmless to tenancy resolution**: §6.1 reads the operator row it already has, and never chases a pointer into a spent parent.

**Why not "no credential at all — just tap your name".** It is the shape the ergonomics argue for and it fails 019 T35(c): *"periodic reconciliation of Longbox-origin sessions against break-glass rows; an unmatched session is K1"*. Attribution that anybody standing at the counter can assert about anybody else is not attribution; it is a label. **022 P1 makes a wrong call the system's defect and never the employee's fault — which is exactly what makes unverified attribution *worse*, not better: a name attached to a book without the person's act is a person who cannot contest it.**

**Why not full re-authentication per book.** 022 P2 requires overrides be cheap; 033 A13 requires no re-login between books; and a friction tax on switching operators is a tax that gets paid by *not switching*, which produces a phone signed in as whoever started the shift and attribution that is silently wrong. **The cheapest thing must be the correct thing.**

**What the operator picker may and may not render, because this is where 022 P3 is easiest to breach by accident.** The picker is an authentication affordance and may show the roster of display names for the shop. It may **not** be ordered by recency or activity, carry a count, a "last used", a badge, a streak or any per-person datum whatsoever, and **the session, the batch and the item may never render an operator's name next to them** — 034 §2.5's *"never rendered next to a session"*. Stated plainly: **a roster is a list of who could be holding the phone; a leaderboard is a list of what they did, and the difference is one sort order.** §11 I7 asserts it.

**And the picker renders only to a live device session** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). The roster of a shop's staff display names is not public information: it is the answer to *"who works here"*, which is a per-shop datum a passer-by on the same Wi-Fi has no claim on. The picker route sits behind the device session like everything else, and I1 asserts the reachable set for a device-only session is exactly *my shops* plus the picker (R8).

### 3.6 The cookie

> **Two cookies, one per principal (R1). `__Host-lb_device` carries the device session's opaque token and lives to the device session's long absolute expiry. `__Host-lb_op` carries the operator session's opaque token and lives to the operator session's short idle expiry. Both: `HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite=Strict; Path=/`, no `Domain`, no `Max-Age` beyond their own session's expiry, and nothing in either but the token.**

- **`__Host-` prefix** — a browser refuses to accept it unless it is `Secure`, has `Path=/` and has **no `Domain`**, which makes the cookie unsettable by a sibling subdomain. This matters concretely: the estate serves many applications under `intentsolutions.io` behind one Caddy (see the estate ops authority), and a cookie without the prefix can be planted by any of them.
- **`SameSite=Strict`, not `Lax`.** `Lax` exists to preserve top-level cross-site *navigation* into a signed-in state, which is a property a linkable web page needs. **This is a phone client for a shop's own staff; there is no cross-site entry flow to preserve**, and §5 leans on `Strict` as one of its three mechanisms.
- **Two cookies or one? Two, reversing v1.0.0 (R1).** The draft chose one, on the ground that *"sending two cookies would let a client present a mismatched pair and require the server to reconcile them, which is a state machine on the wire for no gain."* **The gain is that the two principals have different lifetimes, which was §3.1's whole point** — a device session measured in weeks and an operator session measured in the gap between two customers — and one cookie forces the pair to share the shorter one. A single cookie carrying the operator token means **the phone is signed out of the shop every time the operator session idles out**, and the recovery from that is not a PIN: it is an enrollment. That is 033 A13's *"no re-login, no re-picking the box"* broken by the cookie layer after §3.5 spent a section protecting it.
- **And the mismatched pair is one check, not a state machine.** The operator session's row carries `parent_session_id`; the hook resolves the device cookie's chain and asserts that the operator row's parent resolves to **that same chain** (§3.5's K4 formula does this anyway). **A pair that does not match is one refusal** — both cookies cleared, the client falls back to the device-session flow — and there is no reconciliation, no repair and no third state. Presenting an operator cookie with no device cookie, a device cookie from another device, or two cookies from two different chains are all **the same refusal**, which is §9.3's constant-answer rule applied to a shape §9.3 did not previously cover.
- **The mismatch is a signal, not just an error.** An operator token presented against a device chain it was not issued under is precisely the shape of a cookie lifted from one phone onto another, and it is recorded as an `auth_attempt` failure (§9.1) — the one place where the pairing buys a detection the single-cookie design could not express.

## 4. Decision C — MFA for privileged roles: TOTP now, passkeys deferred, and the reason is recovery

### 4.1 Who

| Role | Primary factor | Second factor | Where it may be established |
|---|---|---|---|
| `owner` | email + password | **required** (TOTP) | any device; **never** an operator session on a shared phone |
| `manager` | email + password | **required** (TOTP) | any device; as above |
| `support_break_glass` | email + password | **required** (TOTP) | Longbox-operated only; 034 §2.7 already requires `effective_until` and `reason` on the grant |
| `operator` | PIN on an enrolled device (§3.5) | **not required** | an enrolled device only |

**Why `operator` is exempt, argued rather than waived.** 022 P6 and P2 make friction a design defect on the scan flow, and an operator's authentication is already two factors in the ordinary sense — possession of an enrolled device and knowledge of a PIN — with the possession factor being a physical object in a shop. **Requiring a phone-based second factor from a person standing at a shared phone is a ceremony that produces a shared authenticator**, which is worse than no second factor because it looks like one.

**And the line that keeps this honest:** an `owner` who is also working the counter holds an operator session by PIN like anybody else, and **that session cannot reach an owner surface**. A privileged action requires a session established by password + TOTP within a freshness window — 022 P3's *"never on a shared or kiosk device without fresh authentication"*, expressed as a property of the session record rather than a rule in a screen.

**RULED at v1.6.0 by E03-D11, which built both halves of that sentence.** The FIRST factor is
`user_credential` (§10.1's M3 remainder, which §10.2 assigned to nobody), and the session it sits in is
a THIRD `app_session` kind — `privileged` — which names a SHOP and a PERSON and names no device, no
device credential and no parent. **It is deliberately NOT device-bound**, because this paragraph's own
table says *"any device"*: binding it to an enrolled phone would make the only place an owner could
invite somebody the shared counter phone this section refuses, which is that refusal made impossible
to obey. **The freshness window is the privileged chain's ABSOLUTE EXPIRY and there is no second
column for it** — the ceiling is carried unchanged across every rotation (§3.3), so it already IS the
elapsed time since both factors were presented, and an `mfa_verified_at` beside it would be a second
thing that can disagree with the first (040 A8's shape). **A privileged route reads its OWN cookie and
never the other two**, so an operator session on the counter phone cannot satisfy one however
privileged its holder is: I11 stops being a check somebody has to write and becomes a property of
which cookie the hook consults. The full argument, the three `NOT NULL`s dropped (not two), and the
third composite foreign key that keeps K5 total on the new chain are 000-docs/057 §4.

⚠ **And the limit of what that discharges, stated where a reader of THIS section will meet it.** The SHAPE is built and tested; **nothing provisions the first factor in a running deployment** — `setPassword` has no route and no CLI (057 §9 R2a) — so the sign-in and the two issuance routes behind it are reachable in a seeded database and not by a shop. Rows 3 and 3a of §12.4 asked for the factor and the session's shape and are discharged; the provisioning surface was nobody's obligation until 057 made it one.

### 4.2 TOTP over passkeys, for a shop owner on a phone

**Passkeys (WebAuthn) are the stronger factor and the correct end state**, and this record says so rather than pretending otherwise: they are phishing-resistant, there is no shared secret to store, and the server holds a public key that is worthless if stolen. TOTP is a shared secret the server must be able to read, and its codes are phishable in real time.

**They are still not the choice for v0, on four grounds, of which the fourth is decisive.**

1. **Enrollment breadth.** A shop owner is one person with one phone and no IT. A passkey's practical cross-device story is platform sync — an iCloud or Google account Longbox cannot see, cannot evidence and does not appear in 046 §7.2's secret inventory or in any processor register (022 P7 caps the pilot at two external processors and E01-B06 owns the terms).
2. **A second browser surface at G2.** WebAuthn is a new client API on a client that today is one `public/app.js` with no build step (E12), and 022 P6 puts a WCAG 2.2 AA obligation on every Longbox-authored view — including, per its own text, **3.3.7 and 3.3.8, which bind E03-B02 by name**. TOTP's UI is a text input.
3. **No fallback that is not a recovery code anyway.** The honest fallback for a lost passkey is a printed recovery code — a shared secret of roughly TOTP's strength with worse ergonomics — so the passkey path does not remove the secret, it adds one.
4. **The failure modes are not comparable, and this is the ground that decides it. A lost TOTP secret is a *recovery event this record can define* (§8). A lost passkey is a *platform we do not control*.** In a one-owner shop with no second administrator, the first is a procedure and the second is an outage whose resolution belongs to a vendor.

> **Decision: TOTP (RFC 6238, 6 digits, 30-second step) is the required second factor for `owner`, `manager` and `support_break_glass`. The schema does not preclude passkeys — `user_authenticator.kind` is a CHECK enum whose only member today is `totp` — and adding `webauthn` as an *additional* factor is a later decision with its own record, never a replacement that removes the recovery path §8 depends on.**

**Where the TOTP secret lives, and the one thing this record will not pretend.** A TOTP secret must be readable by the server, so locked decision 2's *"raw keys never in the database"* — a rule about **provider** credentials named by `key_ref` — cannot be satisfied by indirection here. The decision is: **the secret is stored encrypted at rest with a key that lives in the process environment and never in the database, under a variable the fail-closed config check validates (E13-B02), and the vault addressing is E03-B05's.** This is 047's *"the rule and the encoding are two decisions with different half-lives"* and 046 Q1's SPLIT ruling applied to a new secret: the **rule** (never in the database in the clear, never in a log, never in a fixture) lands now; the **custody** waits for the vault. §12.4 hands it over and §11 I9 asserts the rule half.

**"Encrypted at rest" is not a specification, and here it is one (R18).**

> **AEAD, not a bare cipher: AES-256-GCM or XChaCha20-Poly1305, chosen at build time and recorded. A fresh random nonce per row, stored beside the ciphertext and never reused across rows. The row's `id` is bound in as additional authenticated data (AAD), so a ciphertext lifted from one authenticator row and pasted into another fails to authenticate rather than decrypting to a working secret. And a `key_version smallint NOT NULL` column from day one, on the same row.**

Each clause is there because its absence is a specific, known failure. **AEAD rather than a bare cipher** because an unauthenticated ciphertext in a database is malleable by anyone who can write to the database, and the failure is silent. **A per-row nonce** because GCM nonce reuse under one key is catastrophic rather than degrading. **The row id as AAD** because a TOTP secret's whole meaning is *whose second factor this is*, and an encryption that does not bind the ciphertext to the row binds it to nothing. **`key_version` from day one** because it is a nullable-column-added-later problem otherwise: a rotation with no version column is a rotation that has to guess, and the guess is per-row. Adding it costs two bytes now; adding it during an incident costs a migration on a table the incident is about. **The same construction applies to any AEAD-encrypted column this system later adds**, and E03-B05 inherits it as a floor rather than a suggestion.

**RULED at v1.5.0 by E03-D06, which built the factor and could not build the session.** `migrations/025` lands
`user_authenticator` exactly as R18 specifies — **AES-256-GCM**, a fresh 12-byte nonce per row in its own column, the row's `id`
as AAD (which is why the application mints the id rather than defaulting it: the AAD must be known before the ciphertext is
computed), and `key_version smallint NOT NULL` from day one. The key is `LONGBOX_AUTHENTICATOR_KEY_V<n>`, base64 of exactly 32
bytes, and **the server refuses to boot without one** (`requireAuthenticatorKey`, the posture §9.2's pepper already has). The
rotation model is **050 §4's, borrowed rather than invented**: every version present in the environment can DECRYPT, the HIGHEST
present ENCRYPTS, so introducing `..._V2` is a rotation performed by adding a variable and restarting, no row is touched, and
re-encrypting the old rows is a later FACT-producing job with its own bead. Removing a key before that job runs takes every
second factor still sealed under it — which is stated in `.env.example` rather than discovered, and is exactly the outage §8's
recovery codes exist for.

**BUILT at v1.6.0 by E03-D11: `pnpm reencrypt-authenticators` is that job, and its shape is a decision
rather than a script.** A re-seal RETIRES the predecessor `replaced` and INSERTS a successor sealed
under the current version, in ONE transaction — **it does not edit the sealed column**, because R18
binds the ciphertext to the row's own id and the column is declared immutable by a trigger precisely
so that a MOVED ciphertext fails to authenticate rather than decrypting to a working factor. A
rotation that carved an exception into that sentence would remove the guarantee for every row,
permanently, to save one INSERT. **`last_used_step` is carried forward with the secret**, and that is
the one thing this job can get silently wrong: a successor with a NULL replay guard makes the code the
owner used thirty seconds ago valid again, which is R19's replay reintroduced by maintenance. The
secret itself does not change, so nobody re-scans anything — `.env.example`'s step 2 said *"a fresh
enrollment per person"*, which read as a forced re-enrollment and is corrected. See 000-docs/057 §4.6.

**What it did NOT build, and why that is a finding rather than a gap in the work.** §4.1 places the second factor in *"a session
established by password + TOTP within a freshness window"*. **The FIRST factor is `user_credential`, which §10.1 lists in M3 and
§10.2's table assigns to no bead at all** — and §3.1 ratifies TWO sessions, both bound to an enrolled phone by `app_session`'s
`kind` CHECK and its composite foreign keys, so there is no row shape here for a person on their own laptop. A route shipped
against that would be reachable from exactly one place — an OPERATOR session on the shared counter phone — which §4.1 refuses in
its own words. So E03-D06 ships the factor with **no route**, reached from a CLI, and §12.4 row 3a hands the first factor and the
privileged-session SHAPE to a named bead. **A third session kind is a decision record's work, not a build agent's.**

### 4.3 Verification, replay and rotation

- A code is valid for its own step and one step either side, and **single-use per `(authenticator, step)`**: `last_used_step` is recorded and a re-presented step is refused. Without this, a code observed over a shoulder or in a screenshot is valid for the rest of its window.
- **`last_used_step` moves by a conditional UPDATE, and the affected-row count is the authorization (R19).** *"Record the step and refuse a re-presented one"* is a read-then-write, and a read-then-write on a replay guard is a race: two submissions of the same code both read the old value, both find it lower, both proceed. The statement is `UPDATE user_authenticator SET last_used_step = $new WHERE id = $1 AND (last_used_step IS NULL OR last_used_step < $new)`, and **the verification succeeds only if that statement affected exactly one row.** Zero rows affected means another transaction already consumed this step, and the answer is a refusal, not a retry. **The database decides, not the application** — 041 §4.2(i)'s constraint-over-lock preference in its cheapest form, and the same shape 042 §5.3 uses for the idempotency key: the write *is* the check.
- **RULED at v1.5.0: the lockout anchor is MIRRORED from §9.1, not extended.** §9.1's construction is *"count and verify in one
  transaction under `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` on an anchor row, because every reader of the count is a writer of the row it is a
  count about"*, with `operator_pin` as the anchor for a PIN. A TOTP verification cannot use that anchor: an `operator_pin` row
  is keyed on `(device_id, app_user_id)` and a TOTP verification **has no device**, so there is no pair to lock, and locking the
  person's PIN rows would serialise a second factor behind an unrelated credential on an unrelated phone. **`user_authenticator`
  is the anchor** — the same row the conditional UPDATE above writes, which is what makes the mirror exact rather than merely
  similar — the window is keyed per `app_user_id` (§9.1's own key "for a password"), and **TOTP and recovery-code attempts share
  ONE budget**, because two forms of one factor with two budgets is one budget an attacker doubles by alternating. There is no
  per-device ceiling above it, and the reason is that these attempts have no device: §9.1's device class bounds a roster walk on
  a shared counter phone, which is a PIN attack.
- **Completing MFA rotates the session** (§3.3): the pre-MFA session is spent and a full session is issued, so no token that existed before the second factor is still live after it.
- Enrollment is confirmed by presenting a code from the enrolled secret; a secret that is generated and never confirmed never becomes an authenticator row.

## 5. Decision D — CSRF: no synchronizer token, and three mechanisms that each fail loudly

### 5.1 The rule

> **No CSRF token is issued and none is checked. Cross-site writes are refused by four properties — three that already exist for other reasons, plus one this record adds because three of them are owned elsewhere — and each fails loudly rather than silently: `SameSite=Strict` on `__Host-` cookies; a mandatory non-safelisted request header on every mutating route, checked before the session is read; the total absence of a CORS policy, asserted by a test; and `Sec-Fetch-Site: same-origin` checked in this record's own hook.**

**The fourth mechanism, and why the record adds one after arguing against adding one (R9).** §13 Q4's objection is the sharp one: *"three controls that are each owned by another record and can each be changed by a bead that has never read this one."* Two of the three genuinely are — mechanism (2) is 042's idempotency rule and mechanism (3) is the absence of a dependency — so the count of mechanisms **this record controls** was one. That is the real finding, and the answer is not a synchronizer token (§5.2's argument against it stands untouched) but a check this record owns outright:

> **Every non-`GET`/`HEAD` route asserts `Sec-Fetch-Site: same-origin`, in the authentication hook, BEFORE the session is read. Where the header is absent — an older browser, a non-browser client — the fallback is an `Origin` allowlist against the configured public origin, and an absent `Origin` on a non-safelisted method is a refusal.**

It costs one header comparison, is set by the browser and unsettable by page script, and — unlike a synchronizer token — **it cannot be quietly not-compared**, because the comparison is the hook and the hook is the thing without which no route resolves a tenant (§6.2, I5). It fails loudly on the same axis as the other three.

**And the ops constraint that removes the class rather than checking it, handed to E13.** Every mechanism here is defending against a sibling application under `intentsolutions.io` — the estate serves many behind one Caddy (§3.6). **A dedicated registrable domain for this application removes the sibling-subdomain class outright**: no sibling can set a cookie for it, no sibling shares its origin, and `Sec-Fetch-Site: same-origin` becomes a statement about a boundary the DNS enforces. That is an operational decision with a cost, not a code change, and it is **E13's** — filed in §12.4 with this paragraph as its reason.

### 5.2 The argument

A classic CSRF attack is a cross-site page causing the victim's browser to issue a state-changing request with the victim's cookies. Against this system it must clear four hurdles:

1. **The cookie is `SameSite=Strict`** (§3.6), so a cross-site request carries no session at all and is anonymous — which after this record means refused.
2. **Every mutating route requires an `Idempotency-Key` header** (E13, `routes/scanSessions.ts:39-46`, 042 §5.1). A cross-site HTML form cannot set a custom header; a cross-site `fetch` that sets one is no longer a *simple request* and triggers a CORS preflight.
3. **There is no CORS policy** (§0: `cors|access-control-allow` → exit 1), so the preflight is refused by the browser and the request is never sent.
4. **Every body is parsed by a `.strict()` Zod schema** (E13), so the form-encoded shapes a cross-site form *can* send fail validation before reaching a service.

**Would a synchronizer token add anything?** It would add a fourth mechanism whose characteristic failure is **silent**: a token that is issued, rendered, sent and then not actually compared. Every one of the three above fails visibly — a wrong cookie attribute breaks the operator's own session; a missing header returns `IDEMPOTENCY_KEY_REQUIRED`; a CORS header added by accident is a line in a diff and a failing test. **A control whose absence is invisible is worth less than a control whose absence breaks the happy path**, and this is the same reasoning 043 A3 used against instructions-as-invariants.

### 5.3 The residual, stated rather than buried

- **`SameSite` is a browser control.** A client that is not a browser is outside all three mechanisms. **That is not a hole to patch here; it is the reason a non-browser client must never be given a cookie.** E05-B08's offline queue is the same origin and the same browser; **E18-B02's partner authentication must be a bearer credential on a header, never a cookie**, and §12.4 hands it over with that constraint attached.
- **Mechanism (2) is contingent on a rule another record owns.** The day a mutating route is added that does not require `Idempotency-Key`, this defence silently disappears from that route. **So it is asserted over the route table rather than remembered** (§11 I6): every route whose method is not `GET`/`HEAD` requires the header, checked before authentication so that the cheapest refusal comes first.

  > **Where the check runs, precisely, because "before authentication" is not precise enough (R10).** Today the header requirement lives inside each handler (`routes/scanSessions.ts:39-46`) — which is **after** `@fastify/multipart` has begun consuming the body. A cross-site request that will be refused for a missing header has, by then, already had up to 25 MiB streamed and written to disk (`app.ts:17`, `routes:136`). **The requirement moves to a route-table-driven `onRequest` hook, ahead of the multipart parser and ahead of the session read**, so the refusal costs one header lookup and touches no disk, no database and no session. It is driven from the route table rather than written per handler for the reason I6 exists: a rule enforced by fourteen copies is a rule that has thirteen places to be forgotten. **I6(b) asserts the ordering itself**, not merely the presence of the check — a check that runs in the wrong place is a check that stops being a CSRF defence and becomes a validation error.
- **A GET can still be cross-site-issued.** No `GET` in this system mutates ON THE STRENGTH OF AN AMBIENT CREDENTIAL (E2's route list), and §11 I6's second half asserts it stays that way. *(v1.5.2, E03-B06: the sentence read "No `GET` in this system mutates" flatly, and the clause added here is the ground this bullet was always giving — the danger is that the browser attaches the COOKIE, so a `GET` that reads no cookie carries no authority for a cross-site navigation to spend. An OAuth callback is that shape, universally. See I6(d) and 000-docs/053 §8.3.)*

## 6. Decision E — The session is the only source of `shop_id`

### 6.1 The rule

> **`shop_id` is resolved from the session's memberships, before any handler runs. The `shopId` in the URL is a value to be checked against it, never the tenant itself. A request whose URL shop is not one the session holds is answered exactly as a request for a shop that does not exist.**

This is 046 §9.2's obligation on this bead, quoted rather than re-derived: *"the identity it establishes is the only source of `shop_id` for a request; the path parameter becomes a value to be checked against the session, never the tenant itself"* (034 `034:408`).

The resolved context is 034 §3.1's `RequestContext`, unchanged, and this record fills in where each field comes from: `app_user_id` and `device_id` from the session chain; `shop_id` and `location_id` from the device session's enrollment or from the membership the URL selects; `role` as the highest role held at that scope; `organization_id` derived from the shop; `correlation_id` from the existing hook (`http/errors.ts:99`).

### 6.2 One hook, and its position closes a gap assigned to another bead

The authentication hook is a single `onRequest` inside the tenant plugin (`src/app.ts:75-93`), registered **before** the rate-limit hook. That ordering is not cosmetic:

> **Once the session resolves the shop, the ordinary rate bucket is keyed on the session's `shop_id` rather than on `req.params.shopId` — and 042 §8.1's rule *"per shop, never per IP"* becomes true for the first time.**

046 assigns that fix to E13-B02 *"after G-1"* (G-20). **It is not a separate change: it is `deps.limiter.takeOrdinary(ctx.shopId)` instead of `takeOrdinary(req.params.shopId)`, in a hook this record adds anyway.** E13-B02 keeps the per-process problem (E5's two `Map`s), which is a genuinely different defect and stays where 046 put it.

**Failing closed on hook order.** A route registered outside the tenant plugin never sees the hook, which is exactly how E3 happened. The route walk (`tests/contract/route-table-scoping.test.ts`) already fails closed on an unclassified route; §11 I5 extends it to fail closed on a route that is neither on the auth allowlist nor covered by the authentication hook.

**The AUTH allowlist is a SEPARATE list from the tenancy allowlist (R12).** `src/contracts/v1/routes.ts` already carries a tenancy allowlist with `kind ∈ {exemption, defect}` (042 A8). It is tempting to reuse it — and it is wrong, because **the two lists answer different questions**: *"may this route sit outside the tenant prefix"* and *"may this route be reached without a session"* have different answers on the same route. `GET /healthz` is outside the prefix **and** anonymous. `POST …/operator-sessions` (the PIN) is outside the tenant prefix (it establishes the operator, so it cannot be inside it) and is **not** anonymous — it needs a live device session. **Collapsing them means one of the two answers is inferred, and an inferred security answer is the shape E3 already took once.**

> **Two lists, each fail-closed, each with a reason and a closing bead, and a route absent from both is a build failure. The AUTH list's rows carry the required principal — `none`, `device`, or `device+operator` — never a bare boolean, because "anonymous or not" cannot express the PIN route.**

**The 308 aliases take a redirect-only row (R12).** `src/app.ts:133-134` register two `app.all` handlers that reply 308 and touch nothing (`:129-131`). They are not anonymous *routes* in the sense the list means, and they must not be given the `none` principal, because that reads as "this endpoint serves unauthenticated callers". They get their own `kind: "redirect"` row asserting what they actually are: **a method-and-body-preserving redirect that reads nothing, writes nothing and resolves no tenant.** And the invariant that matters: **I6 asserts that an alias path and its direct `/api/v1` path refuse identically** — same code, same body, same absence of `details` — because a redirect that answers differently from its target is an oracle sitting on the front door with the word "deprecated" over it.

**Membership-first execution, not merely membership-first logic (R13).** §6.1 says `shop_id` is resolved from the session's memberships. It does not say what the resolver may touch on the way, and the difference is an oracle:

> **The path never reads the `shop` table before the membership test passes.** Resolution is a single query rooted at `membership` — `FROM membership JOIN shop ON …` with the `app_user_id` and the live-revocation predicate applied — and never a lookup of the URL's shop followed by a membership check on the result. A shop the caller holds no membership at is a shop whose row this request never loads, so there is no timing difference, no log line and no error path that distinguishes "exists but not yours" from "does not exist". **§6.5 makes the two answer the same; this makes them cost the same**, and the second is what survives an attacker with a stopwatch.

**Rate classes for the routes that have no session yet (R14).** §6.2 moves the ordinary bucket to `ctx.shopId`, which is correct for every route that resolves a tenant — and this record adds four that cannot, because they are how a caller acquires one. 042 §8.1's *"per shop, never per IP"* has nothing to key on there, and leaving them unbucketed makes the front door the one unmetered surface in the system.

| Route class | Rate key | Why not something else |
|---|---|---|
| PIN verification (operator session) | **`device_id`** from the live device session | the device is authenticated; the person is what is being tested. Keying on `app_user_id` lets a stranger exhaust a named person's budget, which is §9.1's *"never a global lock a stranger can trigger against a named person"* arriving as a rate limit instead of a lockout |
| Invitation acceptance | **the shop the token names** | the token names a shop before any session exists; that is the tenant, and it is the shop's own budget being spent |
| Device enrollment | **the shop the enrollment code names** | as above; an enrollment storm is a shop's problem and is bounded by a shop's ceiling |
| Password + TOTP (privileged sign-in) | **`app_user_id`**, with §9.1's lockout as the real control | this is the one place a named-principal key is right, because the lockout is already per person and the rate class is only there to bound cost |

> **Never IP, and never unbounded.** 042 §8.1's reasoning is unchanged and is the reason there is a table here rather than a shrug: one shop is one IP, two shops behind one carrier are one IP, and a bucket a caller chooses is not a bucket (E5). **Every route in the system is now in exactly one class**, and I5's route walk fails closed on a route in none.

### 6.3 `operator_id` and `actor_verified` come from the session, and only from the session

> **Every write records `operator_id` from the session and sets `actor_verified = true`. Neither is ever accepted from a request body. No pre-G2 row is backfilled and no `actor_verified` is ever flipped to true (034 §4.4, `migrations/003:44-51`).**

This is the moment 046 E23 named: the accessor module gets something to gate. **`created_by` and `confirmed_by` stay unwritten (E7) and are never resurrected as a fallback** — a system that writes both a verified id and an unverified string has two attributions and no rule for which is true.

### 6.4 `GET /api/v1/shops` becomes *my shops*, and the tree's last defect row closes

> **The route returns the shops THE SESSION MAY ACT ON, which is the one shop the session pins. For a device session with no operator, that is the shop the device is enrolled to, read by the session's own enrollment id. For a device+operator session, it is that same shop and only when the operator holds a live membership at it, from a query ROOTED AT `membership` joining `shop` (R13). Its allowlist row's `kind` flips from `defect` to `exemption`, and its reason becomes what it now is: the route that establishes which tenant, which therefore cannot sit inside the tenant prefix.**

**AMENDED at v1.3.0 (E03-D08 invariant review, acting head).** This paragraph read *"the shops the caller holds a live membership at"*, which for an operator with memberships at two shops would list both. It is **narrowed to the pinned shop**, on two grounds. First, §3.5 pins `shop_id`/`location_id` on the device session and denormalizes them onto the operator session, so **every other shop already answers `SHOP_NOT_FOUND`** (§6.5) — a picker listing one would offer an entry whose every use 404s. Second, and the reason this is a security narrowing rather than a UI one: listing it **discloses the name of another shop that person works at** to whoever is holding this shop's counter phone, and a per-person datum on an authentication surface is exactly what 022 P3 and 019 T35 keep off it. The DTO stays an ARRAY, so a genuinely multi-shop session needs no wire change.

> **The consequence, stated: an operator with memberships at two shops cannot switch shops from the picker, and that is a product decision, not a display narrowing.** Switching shops is a **device re-enrollment** (§7.3): the phone is the thing bound to a shop, so moving a person between shops means moving or re-enrolling the phone. There is no in-app shop switch for anybody, and this record is not inventing one at the picker. That is right for a counter phone that lives in one store and wrong for a roaming owner with two storefronts — **the roaming case is not served by v0**, and it is **E03-D07's** to reconsider when enrollment gets its screens rather than something to bolt onto a list endpoint.

**RULED at v1.4.0 by E03-D07, which built enrollment and does NOT serve the roaming case.** The hand-off above says "E03-D07's to reconsider when enrollment gets its screens", and E03-D07 has no screens: the redemption routes are its surface and every view they imply is **E05's** (§12.4). So the reconsideration cannot happen in this bead, and pretending otherwise would leave the case owned by a bead that has closed. **Two rulings, so it is not left hanging.** (1) **The roaming case stays REFUSED for v0, and E03-D07's build strengthens the reason rather than weakening it**: a device session is what pins the shop, the enrollment code names one `location_id` (034 I7, `NOT NULL`), and the credential secret is discarded at redemption so the phone holds only a session bound to that shop — a "switch shops" affordance would have to re-authenticate the phone against a second shop, which is a second enrollment by any other name. (2) **The reconsideration is filed on `E05-B03`** — 014 §8's *"Decide PWA/native shell, camera/scanner APIs, secure local storage, updates and device enrollment"* — which is the bead that owns the enrollment screens the hand-off was actually waiting for. It is a named owner rather than a closed one, and it is the last time this record moves the case.

**The two branches are not one query, and only one is membership-rooted.** A device-only session names no person and therefore holds no membership, so its answer is read by the **session's own enrollment id** — `WHERE shop.id = $sessionShopId`, a fact the session already carries and the caller never supplied, which is why there is nothing to enumerate and no oracle to build. R13's membership-first EXECUTION binds the **operator** branch, where an identity exists to be tested: `FROM membership m JOIN shop s ON s.id = m.shop_id WHERE m.app_user_id = $1 AND m.shop_id = $2 AND <live> AND NOT EXISTS (revocation)`, never a lookup of the shop followed by a membership check on the result.

Two consequences worth naming. **First, this is the whole of 046's rank-1 G-2 half that belongs to this bead** — the RBAC matrix and the T24 runtime assertion remain E03-B03's. **Second, it unblocks G-11**: 042 §3.4's *"no defect-kind row may exist at G2"* has no mechanism today (046 E4), and a G2 assertion that the `defect` count is zero cannot be written as a passing test until the last defect row is gone. **The assertion is still E03-B03's to write; this record is what makes writing it possible.**

### 6.5 A wrong tenant answers `SHOP_NOT_FOUND`, and no new code is added

> **A URL naming a shop the session does not hold is answered with the registry's existing `SHOP_NOT_FOUND` (`errors.ts:84`), with no `details`. No forbidden/unauthorized code is added to the registry.**

A distinct "you may not access this shop" answer confirms the shop exists, which is an enumeration oracle over exactly the table E3's route enumerates today. 019 T24 signs **cross-tenant access** at zero and marks it non-waivable; existence is access to the fact of existence. And 042 §4.3's rule that the server never writes operator prose does the rest of the work: the code carries developer English from the `MESSAGES` map and the client selects operator copy from the code, so the two cases are indistinguishable on the wire **and** on the screen.

**The cost, stated:** an operator who mistypes a shop id gets *"no shop with that id"* rather than *"you are not a member of that shop"*, which is less helpful. It is a URL the operator never types (E12's picker becomes §6.4's *my shops*), and the honest trade is that a support conversation is cheaper than an oracle.

## 7. Decision F — Invitations and device enrollment, delivered out of band (no pilot participant's name)

### 7.1 The rule

> **An invitation is an append-only fact naming a shop, a role, an inviter and an expiry, with `sha256(token)` stored and the token shown once. It is single-use because `invitation_acceptance` carries `UNIQUE (invitation_id)` — not because a status column says so. Acceptance grants the membership in the same transaction.**

The shape is 034 §2.7's grant/release idiom and 047 §5.1's no-status-column rule, and it is chosen for a reason specific to invitations: **a `status` column on an invitation is a column two concurrent acceptances can both read as `pending`**, where a UNIQUE constraint makes the second one a failed INSERT. Single-use is a constraint or it is a race.

**The section header says *"no pilot participant's name"* and means it** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). This section describes how a person is added to a shop. No worked example anywhere in this record, in any test fixture it implies, or in any screenshot of the flow may carry a real shop's name, a real employee's name, or a real invitation code. 022 P7 and the pilot-consent rule in CLAUDE.md both bear on it, and an invitation flow is exactly the surface where a "realistic" example becomes a person's name in a repository.

### 7.1a The token, its entropy, and the device it may be redeemed on (R15)

v1.0.0 stored `sha256(token)` and said the token is *"shown once"*. It did not say how large the token is, and it did not say **where** it may be presented — and the second omission is the load-bearing one, because an invitation is by construction a credential that grants a membership to whoever holds it.

> **An invitation or enrollment code is redeemable ONLY on a device that already holds a live device session enrolled to the same shop the token names.** A correct token presented from any other context — another shop's device, a browser with no device session, a laptop — is refused with §9.3's constant answer.

This is §3.5's argument applied one layer earlier: **a bearer token becomes a possession-bound token when the channel is bound**, and the channel here is a phone behind the counter of the shop the invitation is for. It closes the case that otherwise ends the discussion — a code read aloud across the counter and overheard, or photographed off the owner's screen, being redeemed in a car park.

> **Entropy, stated rather than left to the build.** Either: **128 bits from a CSPRNG, base32-encoded, pasted or scanned** — the code is never typed, so its length costs nothing; **or** a short human-readable code (the six-to-eight characters the counter flow actually wants), permitted **only** with the device binding above **and** a per-shop ceiling on outstanding codes and on redemption attempts (§6.2's rate class). **A short code without both is refused.** The two are a genuine choice for the build to make against the counter's ergonomics; neither is a choice to make silently.

**RULED at v1.4.0 by E03-D07, which built both, and the two answers differ.** An **invitation** takes the short form (eight Crockford-base32 characters, ~40 bits) because both conditions above hold and are enforced in code a test can fail. An **enrollment code** takes **128 bits**, because the binding above is not merely unmet but *unsatisfiable*: the caller of an enrollment IS the phone being enrolled, so it holds no session by construction — §7.3's own sentence is *"the phone posts it once and receives a device session"* — and "a short code without both is refused" therefore governs. A second reason this paragraph did not anticipate: **a wrong enrollment guess names no shop**, so the per-shop attempt ceiling has nothing to key on until the guess is already correct and cannot bound a guessing attack there at all. The full argument is in `src/services/auth/codes.ts` and `enrollment.ts`; the change-log row records it.

> **`device_enrollment_code_use` carries `UNIQUE (code_id)`**, exactly as `invitation_acceptance` carries `UNIQUE (invitation_id)`. v1.0.0 named the constraint for invitations and left enrollment codes described as *"one-time"* in prose — and *"one-time"* in prose is the status column §7.1 just refused, wearing an adjective. **Single-use is a constraint on both tables or it is a race on one of them.**

**This settles §13 Q8**, and §13.0 records why: the question asked at what shop size the out-of-band flow stops being right, and the answer is that with the device binding in place, the flow's limit is not a *size* — it is the arrival of a shop whose staff are enrolled somewhere other than at the counter, which is E13's delivery decision and not a threshold this record can name.

### 7.2 There is no email service, and this record will not invent one

**§0's grep is exit 1**: no mailer, no SMS client, no transport of any kind, and none in `.env.example`. So the v0 delivery is stated rather than assumed:

> **The owner delivers the invitation code to the employee in person. The screen shows it once; the employee enters it on the enrolled device and sets a PIN.**

**This is not a placeholder for a real channel, and it is arguably stronger than one.** An invitation delivered across the counter to a person whose face the owner knows is authenticated by a channel no email provider can match; an emailed invitation makes an inbox the root of trust for a shop's catalog and adds a processor that sees a credential in transit — which 022 P7's two-processor pilot cap and E01-B06's processor terms both have to account for. **The transactional-delivery provider choice is routed to E13** (§12.4), with the criteria written down here rather than discovered there: it is a processor under 022 P7; it sees credentials in transit; and it must not become the recovery path §8 deliberately declines to build.

### 7.3 Device enrollment

An `owner` or `manager`, in a privileged session (§4.1), creates a **one-time, short-lived, shop-scoped enrollment code** naming a location. The phone posts it once and receives a device session; the `device` and `device_credential` rows are written in that transaction. 034 §2.8's `device.location_id` is `NOT NULL` (its I7), so an enrollment names a location or fails.

**A device session is long-lived and revocable, and the lost-phone case is the design target.** Revoking `device_credential` (034's `device_credential_revocation`) kills the device session and every operator session whose `parent_session_id` names it, on the next request — which is §3.3's derivation doing its job without a sweep.

### 7.4 One amend-by-a-row this record PROPOSES on a ratified record

034 §2.8 gives `device_credential` a `key_ref text NOT NULL` described as *"the NAME of a reference, never a key or a public key blob — the `shop_credentials:34-35` rule, applied verbatim (locked decision 2)"*.

**The rule was applied verbatim to an object it does not fit.** `shop_credentials.key_ref` names an **environment variable an operator provisions** — and `migrations/014` has since narrowed it to a `LONGBOX_<SEGMENT>_<REST>` shape bound to the shop's slug (E9). A device credential is **minted by the system, one per phone**, and there is no environment variable for it to name; satisfying the rule literally would mean an env var per enrolled device, which is unimplementable at the second phone.

> **Proposed: 034 takes an amend-by-a-row to v1.2.0 replacing `device_credential.key_ref` with `token_hash text NOT NULL` — `sha256` of a 256-bit system-minted secret — with the reason recorded. A hash of a system-minted secret is not a key: it opens nothing, and it is the same object `app_session` stores (§3.2).**

Locked decision 2 is untouched: it governs **provider** credentials, every one of which still names an environment variable and none of which is stored. **§14 is signed, so this amendment is APPLIED**: 034 goes to v1.2.0 in the same commit as this ratification, following 047's handling of its own 030 amendment, and it carries a 006 row of the same date.

**And the residual the amendment creates, stated rather than left implicit** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). Replacing an env-var name with a `token_hash` makes the device credential **bearer material sitting in a browser cookie jar on a phone that lives on a shop counter**. That is a real downgrade from a public-key credential and it is accepted with its eyes open:

- The device secret is **not** a second factor and must never be described as one. It authenticates *an app instance*, which is a claim about a phone and not about a person.
- Its compensating controls are the ones §3 and §7.3 already carry: it is `HttpOnly` and `__Host-`-prefixed so no page script and no sibling subdomain can read it; it is revocable in one row, killing every operator session above it at the next request; and **on its own it reaches exactly *my shops* and the operator picker** (R8, I1) — a stolen device cookie with no PIN is a browser that can see which shop the phone belongs to and a list of display names, and can write nothing.
- **What it does not have is phishing resistance or hardware binding.** A passkey-style device credential (a keypair in the phone's secure element, attested at enrollment) is the correct end state and is the same argument §4.2 makes about the owner's second factor, blocked on the same platform dependency. **It is filed for E03-B05 alongside the vault**, not left as an unstated known-better.

## 8. Decision G — Recovery: owner-only, code-first, and out of band by design

### 8.1 The rule

> **Recovery codes are the primary path: at MFA enrollment the owner receives a set of single-use codes, shown once, stored with argon2id. Using one is an append-only fact and forces MFA re-enrollment. There is no self-service email or SMS reset, because there is no email or SMS service (§0) and because introducing one here would make an inbox the root of trust for a shop's catalog.**

**A recovery code substitutes for the SECOND factor only — never for the password (R20).** v1.0.0 called recovery codes *"the primary path"* without saying what they replace, and the reading that gets built is *"a recovery code signs you in"*. That reading turns a printed slip in a drawer into a **single-factor bearer credential for the owner account** — strictly worse than the password it would be bypassing, because it is written down by design and cannot be changed by the person who memorised it.

> **The recovery flow is: email + password (unchanged, still required) + one unused recovery code, in place of the TOTP code. A recovery code is never accepted without the password, is never accepted on its own, and is never accepted in place of the password.** The code is a second factor with worse ergonomics and no clock, and that is exactly what it is for: the case where the clock is on a phone at the bottom of a lake.

> **Using one forces MFA re-enrollment, and this is a rule rather than a prompt.** The resulting session is a **re-enrollment session**: it can establish a new TOTP authenticator and issue a new recovery-code set, and it can reach **nothing else** until it has. Otherwise the owner works for six months on recovery codes, the second factor quietly becomes a stack of paper, and the first time anybody notices is when the paper runs out. **Re-enrollment retires every remaining code in the old set** — an append-only `recovery_code_use` fact plus a superseding set, never an edit — because codes that survive a re-enrollment are codes that survive whatever caused it.

**RULED at v1.6.0 by E03-D11, on 055 §9 item 4a: the codes KEEP the pepper (branch (b)), and the
consequence 055 requires to be written in terms is written here.**

> **A recovery code is a fallback for a LOST PHONE and never for a CUSTODY LOSS.** It is hashed with
> the same process-environment pepper as the PIN and the password (§9.2), so a pepper loss takes it
> along with everything else. The 055 cannon RECOMMENDED branch (a) — 128-bit codes hashed without the
> pepper — and it is declined on R20's own ground: a code is never accepted without the password, the
> password is peppered, so unpeppering the code buys **no** pepper-loss survivability while trading
> away §8.1's ratified ergonomics (*"a 26-character string on a slip in a drawer is a string that gets
> photographed instead"*). What the cannon was right about is that the dependency was INHERITED by
> function delegation rather than decided; it is decided now, with its argument on the function. The
> way back from a custody loss is 055 §6.1's runbook, and no artifact may say otherwise.

**And the re-enrollment session it yields is REACHABLE but nearly empty**, which is stated rather than
implied: E03-D11 makes `must_reenroll` a predicate over facts, checked on every privileged request and
not only at sign-in, so a factor retired from another surface puts the session in that state too. **It
reaches only its own sign-out today**, because there is no enrollment ROUTE — enrolling is still `pnpm
enroll-authenticator`, a schema-owner CLI, and 000-docs/057 §9 R2 names the bead that closes it.

**RULED at v1.5.0 by E03-D06: recovery codes are issued to all three MFA-REQUIRED roles, not only to `owner`.** §8's heading
says *"owner-only"* and the question it was answering is the one §8.3 states — the contrast is with **STAFF**, whose PIN reset
*"needs no channel at all"* because the owner does it in person. A `manager` and a `support_break_glass` operator are required to
hold a second factor by §4.1 and lose their phones exactly as often; a second factor with no recovery path for two of the three
roles that must hold it is an outage whose only exit is §8.2's break-glass. So the set is issued at every enrollment, and
`enrollAuthenticator` refuses a person whose only live role is `operator` — which is §4.1's exemption enforced rather than
assumed.

**The parameters, each a PROVISIONAL floor (042 A3) with its derivation stated and never quoted as a security property.** EIGHT
codes per set: enough that an owner who uses one is not on their last, few enough that verifying a WRONG code — which tries the
live codes in turn, because §8.1 fixes argon2id and a digest carries no label — costs eight runs rather than forty. TEN Crockford
base32 characters (~50 bits), and the choice is against a **printed slip**, not against §7.1a's redemption codes: this one is
typed by one person under stress, is worthless without the password (R20), is single-use by constraint, is stored under argon2id
with the process pepper, and faces an online path bounded by §9.1's growing delay — while a 26-character string on a slip in a
drawer is a string that gets photographed instead, which is a worse custody story than four fewer bits. **Liveness is a
predicate** — newest `batch_id`, no `recovery_code_use` row — so §8.1's *"re-enrollment retires every remaining code in the old
set"* is the arrival of a new batch and not a column anybody has to remember to write.

### 8.2 What happens when the codes are gone too

The residual case — an owner with no phone and no codes — is handled by what already exists rather than by a new mechanism:

> **A Longbox-operated recovery under the existing manual break-glass runbook.** 022 P7: *"at Pilot A every Longbox access to shop data is recorded as a 006 row naming operator, reason and expiry (a manual break-glass runbook), evidenced at least once before G3."* The recovery is a `support_break_glass` membership (034 §2.7, which already requires `effective_until` and `reason` by CHECK), it issues a single-use recovery grant, and it is an append-only fact **the owner sees on their next sign-in**.

**The cheapest fix for the residual case is not a mechanism at all** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). Every path in §8.2 exists because a one-owner shop has no second person who can vouch for the first. **So the registration flow asks for one.**

> **At shop registration the owner nominates either a second `owner`, or a named recovery contact recorded on the shop.** A second owner is the strictly better answer — it is a person with their own factors who can restore the first without Longbox touching anything — and it is available to more shops than the pilot's shape suggests: 034 §2.6 sources `manager` to the 19% with a second storefront, but a spouse, a partner or a business co-owner is not a storefront. **A named recovery contact is the fallback for the shop where it genuinely is one person**: it is not a credential and grants nothing, it is the out-of-band identity check E11-B09's runbook performs against, so the verification stops being *"the person who emailed sounds right"*.
>
> **It is nominated, not required.** A shop that declines proceeds, and the decline is a fact on the shop row rather than a blank field — because the difference between *"this owner has no second person"* and *"nobody asked"* is the difference between a known residual and a surprise during an outage. **The nomination is a 038/039 charter fill-in item**, filed there rather than invented here.

**The identity check is out of band and is not this record's to invent**: the shop signed a pilot charter with a named signer (038 §9, 039), and the verification procedure is E11-B09's operational runbook. **What this record fixes is that the technical path is the audited one and not a second, quieter path** — there is no super-admin, no "reset password" affordance for Longbox staff, and no way to obtain an owner session except by holding the owner's factors or by an audited, expiring, reasoned grant.

### 8.3 Staff PIN reset needs no channel at all

The owner resets it in person, in a privileged session, and the employee sets a new PIN on the device. **The whole out-of-band problem for the ninety-percent case is solved by the fact that these people work in the same room.**

### 8.4 Four mechanisms explicitly refused

| Refused | Why |
|---|---|
| **Security questions** | a second password with lower entropy, drawn from facts a shop's own social media answers |
| **SMS as a factor or a reset channel** | SIM-swap is the documented failure mode for exactly this population, and it is a third processor against 022 P7's two-processor pilot cap |
| **"Email the owner a magic link"** | no email service exists (§0); building one to serve recovery makes an inbox the root of trust for the catalog, and the provider sees the credential |
| **A break-glass role that can mint an owner session silently** | 034 §2.6: *"`support_break_glass` is never granted at ratification and never grants itself"*; 022 P7: *"there is no invisible super-admin"* |

## 9. Decision H — Brute force, lockout, and answering identically

### 9.1 Attempts are facts, not counters in a process

> **`auth_attempt` is append-only and records FAILURES ONLY: the principal attempted, the device, the method, the failure class and the time. Lockout is a derivation over it — per `(device_id, app_user_id)` for a PIN, per `app_user_id` for a password — that increases delay and NEVER closes a door.**

The alternative is E5/046 E25's shape, and its three properties are already documented in this repository: per-process, empty on restart, keyed on something a caller chose. **A lockout with those properties is not a lockout; it is a lockout per worker.** The write cost is one row per **failed** attempt, bounded by the lockout itself.

**Failures only, and successes deliberately not recorded (R16).** v1.0.0 wrote *"successes are recorded too, because T35(c)'s reconciliation of sessions against break-glass rows needs both sides"* — and that is false on its own terms. **T35(c) reconciles Longbox-origin *sessions* against break-glass grants, and the session table is `app_session`**, which is append-only, which records every issuance, and which I14 already walks. A success row in `auth_attempt` would be a **second, redundant record of the same event**, differing from the first only in being a per-operator log of when each person signed in — which is the surface 022 P3 forbids, built to satisfy a reconciliation that does not need it. **The reconciliation derives from `app_session`; `auth_attempt` holds only what went wrong.**

**Lockout degrades to delay and never to a terminal lock (R5, the security lens's D4).**

> **The derivation returns a required-wait, growing with the count of recent failures for the pair, and it never returns "locked". There is no state from which a correct PIN is refused; there is only a state in which it is refused *until*.**

The reason is 022 P1 and the counter. A shared phone in a shop mid-batch, with a queue at the till, and a terminal lock means the operator's work stops until an owner in a privileged session intervenes — **so the security control's failure mode is the shop's revenue**, and a control whose failure mode is revenue is a control that gets disabled. An increasing delay makes brute force impossible on any timescale that matters and leaves the honest fat-fingered operator waiting seconds. **The blunt version: a lockout on a shared counter phone must be a delay that grows and never a door that closes.**

> **A per-`device_id` ceiling sits above the per-pair one.** The per-pair delay alone lets an attacker holding the phone walk the roster — six failures on each of eight display names is eight fresh budgets. The device-wide class is slower to trigger and slower to decay, and it is what actually bounds a stolen-phone attack.
>
> **The DoS trade, stated.** Both classes mean **a coworker can slow another operator down by mistyping their PIN**, and the device ceiling means a coworker can slow the whole phone down. That is accepted, and it is why neither class closes: the worst outcome available to a griefer is a wait, not a shift-ending lock, and §9.1's original clause — *"never a global lock a stranger can trigger against a named person"* — is what forbids the alternative. **A per-`app_user_id` lock spanning devices is refused for this reason**, and only the password class (which is not a counter-phone credential) is keyed per person at all.

**The count and the verify are one transaction, under a lock (R5).** *"Derive the lockout, then verify"* is a read-then-write across a security boundary, and it is the write-skew shape 041 §4.2 and §4.3 already named: N concurrent attempts all read a count below the threshold, all proceed, and the effective budget is N times the intended one.

> **The sequence takes `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` on the `operator_pin` row for that `(device_id, app_user_id)` before counting, and holds it through the verification and the failure INSERT.** This is 041 §4.2's anchor pattern with `operator_pin` as the anchor — the same construction 041 §4.3 chose for `retention_hold` placement, and for the same reason: **the anomaly is write skew, and the fix is that every reader of the count is a writer of the row it is a count about.**
>
> **The window count reads `now()`, and that is a declared exception (R5).** 041 §2.6 sanctions a derivation reading the clock **only by naming it**: 040 §3.4's `abandoned` predicate is the first sanctioned exception, 041 §5.3's `session_seq` the second. **This is the third, and it is sanctioned here by being named**: a lockout window is *a statement about now* in exactly `abandoned`'s sense — "how many times has this pair failed in the last interval" is not replayable and is not meant to be, and its instant is an input. An unnamed clock read would be the bug; this one is on the list.

**Substrate versus surface, in terms a test can decide (R17).** §13 Q9 asks whether `auth_attempt` is a T35 hazard or a T35 control, and *"it is the audit substrate"* is an assertion nobody can falsify. The distinction is written as a rule instead:

> **SUBSTRATE:** a table read **only** by (i) the liveness/lockout derivation, for exactly one `(device_id, app_user_id)` pair, in the request that is authenticating that pair; or (ii) an audited break-glass query under 034 §3.3's accessor, recorded as a 006 row naming operator, reason and expiry.
> **SURFACE:** any read in which an operator identifier appears as a **dimension** — a `GROUP BY`, an `ORDER BY`, a filter parameter, a column in a view, a count over more than one pair, a report, an export, or a response DTO.
> **`auth_attempt` is substrate. Any query over it that is not (i) or (ii) is a surface, and building one is an architecture-gate failure rather than a review finding** — the same enforcement 034 §3.3 A5 gave the other three column names. **I7 names `auth_attempt` explicitly** alongside `operator_id`, `created_by` and `confirmed_by`, because a rule that lists three of four things is a rule about the fourth that nobody wrote down.

**`auth_attempt` is per-operator data and goes behind 034 §3.3's accessor with the other three column names** — this is the record that finally gives that module something to gate (E8), and adding a fourth surface without putting it there would be the exact leak 034 §3.3 A5 closed for the legacy strings. **And it takes the shortest retention window in the system**, filed as an obligation on **E03-B09**: it is failure data about named people, its only legitimate reader is a lockout derivation over a window measured in minutes, and every day it is kept past that window is a day it is a liability rather than a control.

### 9.2 Password and PIN verification

argon2id for both, with parameters set at build time and recorded as **PROVISIONAL floors** (042 A3) rather than quoted as a security property. The split 046 K-2 and `migrations/014` established is reused verbatim (E9): **the database enforces the shape, the function enforces the binding.** A PIN hash is meaningless without its `(device, app_user)` scope, and that is a two-table fact no CHECK can express — so the verification function refuses an out-of-scope pair **in its own body**, before any hash is computed.

**A process-environment pepper is mixed into both (R6).**

> **PIN and password hashes are computed over `secret ‖ pepper`, where the pepper is a high-entropy value read from the process environment at boot — never stored in the database, never in a migration, never in a fixture, never in a backup of this database.** It is the same custody posture §4.2 gives the authenticator key and the same one 041 A5 gives the purge-epoch key: *"held outside the database"*, so a database compromise alone is not a credential compromise.

For a password the pepper is defence in depth. **For the PIN it is close to load-bearing**, and that is the reason it is a decision rather than a build detail: a six-digit PIN has a million-entry keyspace, so an attacker holding a `pg_dump` can enumerate every PIN offline against argon2id in time that is merely expensive rather than impossible — **§3.5's whole "possession-bound channel" argument evaporates the moment the verification can be done offline**, because a dump is not a phone behind a counter. The pepper puts the keyspace out of reach of a stolen dump alone.

**It rotates like a key, not like a password**: peppering is versioned (`pepper_version` beside the hash, the same construction as §4.2's `key_version`), and a rotation re-peppers each credential at its next successful verification rather than by a mass rewrite of a table nobody can decrypt. **The two-secret failure mode is stated:** losing the pepper invalidates every PIN and password in the system, which makes it a backup-and-custody obligation on **E03-B05**, filed there in §12.4 rather than assumed to be somebody's habit.

**RE-HOMED and ANSWERED at v1.6.0 by E03-D11, because the obligation above was ORPHANED.** §12.4 row 3
handed the pepper's custody to E03-D06 → E03-B05, and 055 §2 E5 found that E03-B05's record — 050 —
**does not contain the word "pepper" once**. 000-docs/057 §5 answers it in four parts: what is lost
(every PIN, every password and every recovery code, simultaneously, in every shop, with no in-band
exit), who holds it (055 RULING 1 adopts escrow for all three loss-bearing secrets), where the escrow
is (**it DOES NOT EXIST YET** — 055 RULING 4 records the accepted-risk interim and its cohort
boundary), and the rotation SHAPE — a versioned ring on 050 §4's model, re-peppering at next successful
verification, **decided there and not built**, with its bead named.

**One pepper, now for THREE credentials** (055 §9 item 11, confirmed): this section's *"both"* is the
PIN and the password, and the recovery code has always been the third by inheritance and is now the
third by decision (§8.1's ruling above). **The blast radius therefore GREW in E03-D11**, because
`user_credential` exists and did not before — 055 §6's *"and (once E03-D11 lands) every password"* is
now true rather than anticipated.

### 9.3 Every failure answers the same thing

An unknown email, a wrong password, a wrong PIN, a locked-out account, an unknown invitation token, a spent one and an expired one each produce **one code from the existing registry with no `details`**, and the same work is done in each case so the timing does not distinguish them. This is `http/errors.ts:44-71`'s existing discipline (E11) applied to a new class: *"maps everything unrecognised to `INTERNAL_ERROR` and drops the thrown message rather than forwarding it."* **A helpful error at an authentication boundary is a query interface over the user table.**

**The residual §9.3 does not close, stated rather than left to be discovered (v1.2.0, from the E03-D09 invariant review).** *"The same work is done in each case"* holds for every branch that TESTS a credential — an unknown pair, a retired PIN and a wrong PIN all verify a digest, and the decoy makes the first two cost what the third costs. **It does not hold for a pair that is inside its lockout delay**, which is refused BEFORE any hash is computed: that answer returns in single-digit milliseconds where the others take the argon2id parameters' worth, so **the body withholds "this pair has recent failures" and the clock does not.**

Hashing while blocked would close it and is REFUSED: it hands an attacker a way to make the server spend argon2id at will, which is the denial-of-service surface §3.2 declined to build for the session token and would be worse here because the attacker chooses the rate. **What bounds the leak is the lockout itself** — the fact recovered is one bit about a pair the caller already named, on a channel that is already possession-bound (§3.5), and the delay that produces the timing is the same delay that stops the guessing the bit would inform. It is recorded as a known residual rather than mitigated, in the same register as §3.5's insider paragraph: **a control that answers faster when it refuses harder is a control whose refusal is legible, and this one's is.**

### 9.4 What is deliberately not built

No CAPTCHA (a third processor, and a per-IP control against 042 §8.1). No IP-based blocking (E5's reasoning: one shop is one IP). No "impossible travel" or device-fingerprint heuristic — **a behavioural detector over a shared counter phone is a per-operator surface arriving by the back door**, which is 022 P3's CFO constraint: *"the cheapest way to satisfy T35 is never to build a per-operator surface, and no such surface may be built and then restricted."*

**M2 carries the composite FK, and it is mandatory rather than preferred (K5).** 041 R1 ratified that a successor sits in the same scope as its predecessor, and 041 A2 closed the question by making the constraint **mandatory** wherever it is expressible, on the ground that *"a non-waivable T24 boundary may not sit behind a mechanism §9 exists because it is bypassable"* — a trigger has a `tgenabled`; a foreign key does not.

> **`app_session` carries `FOREIGN KEY (rotated_from, app_user_id, device_id) REFERENCES app_session (id, app_user_id, device_id)`, with the redundant `UNIQUE (id, app_user_id, device_id)` that makes it expressible.**

The point is 041 R1's exactly: **a rotation may not change who the session is for or which device it is on.** Without it, `rotated_from` is a bare pointer, and a bug or a hostile write that chains one person's session onto another's is a **silent privilege transfer** that every liveness check in §3.3 would happily accept — it is a live row with an unrevoked chain and no successor. This is the strongest constraint in the record and it costs one index. **The lock, the transaction and the grace window all assume it**: K2's rotation race resolves by comparing bindings, and a comparison the database does not also enforce is a comparison an attacker writes around.

**Which tables are immutable and which are config**, following 034 §4.2's split (*"a record of something that happened is immutable; a statement about the present is corrected in place"*): **immutable** — `app_session`, `app_session_revocation`, `invitation`, `invitation_acceptance`, `device_enrollment_code`, `device_enrollment_code_use`, `auth_attempt`, `recovery_code_use`. **Config, mutable, and each a declared exemption with a reason row** — `user_credential`, `operator_pin`, `user_authenticator` (whose `last_used_step` moves by the conditional UPDATE of R19, and whose `key_version`/`pepper_version` move on rotation) (a password is changed in place; its *change* is an `auth_attempt` fact, and versioning the hash would keep every old password's hash forever, which is a liability rather than an audit trail).

**BUILT at v1.6.0 by E03-D11 (`migrations/031`), and "config" is narrowed to what the sentence above
actually licenses.** `user_credential` is mutable for exactly three columns — `password_hash`,
`pepper_version`, `updated_at` — and the licence is a MECHANISM rather than a sentence: a
column-scoped GRANT **plus** an `ENABLE ALWAYS` trigger, in the same migration, because a
column-scoped grant with no matching trigger is what E03-D06 shipped on `user_authenticator` and what
PR #82's re-review then had to record as an open replay. That residual is closed in the same file, on
the same table, by the same instrument. **One correction to the parenthesis above**: a password change
is NOT an `auth_attempt` fact — R16 made that table failures-only, and a SUCCESSFUL verification is
recorded nowhere at all, which is 022 P3 working rather than a gap. What the row carries instead is
`updated_at`.

## 12. Alternatives, consequences, and what this record does not decide

### 12.1 Alternatives considered

**A1 — Buy identity: an external IdP (Auth0, Clerk, WorkOS, Supabase Auth).** *Rejected, and it is the alternative with the strongest case.* It would deliver everything in §3, §4, §7 and §8 in an afternoon, by people who do this full time, with passkeys included. Four things rule it out for v0 and none of them is "not invented here" — **and they are re-ordered at ratification so the decisive one leads.**

**(b) is the ground, and it leads because it is the only one that is not a preference.** The construction no hosted IdP will sell is §3.5's: a **device** principal with a PIN-derived **operator** session on top of it, on a phone three people share, where switching operator is one tap and no re-login, and where the device's liveness and the person's liveness are separate facts with separate lifetimes (K4's formula). Every hosted IdP models *a person on a device they own*. The shared-counter-phone shape is the one thing this record exists to get right, it is 022 P3 and 033 A13 reconciled, and **it is unbuyable, not merely inconvenient to buy.** *(v1.0.0 led with the processor cap, which is a policy constraint that a policy decision could lift. Leading with a liftable ground made the strongest alternative look refused on a technicality.)*

**(c)** `operator_id` must be a real FK into `app_user` in *this* database (034 §2.13), so the user table exists locally regardless and the IdP becomes a second source of truth for the same person. **(d)** It moves an availability dependency onto the scan flow: a shop cannot list books because a vendor is down. **(a)** It is a processor holding the shop's operator identities, against 022 P7's two-processor pilot cap and E01-B06's terms — and it would spend one of the two on a component that touches no shop data.

> **And the cost of building, stated as plainly as the four grounds against buying** (RECOMMENDED, adopted). Rejecting A1 means **one engineer, for as long as this product exists, maintaining an authentication system**: argon2id parameters that need revisiting, a pepper and an AEAD key that need custody and rotation (§4.2, §9.2), a TOTP implementation, a lockout, a session lifecycle with a lock on the hot path, and — the part that is not a feature list — **the obligation to notice, alone, when one of them becomes wrong.** A vendor does that noticing as its product. §12.2 already records that this is the largest subsystem in E03 and on the critical path of five others; **this line records the part that does not end when the subsystem ships.** The four grounds still win, and Q1's ruling in §13.0 names the condition under which they would stop winning.
**A2 — One session, with the operator switch as a field on it.** *Rejected*; §3.1. It makes 022 P3's fresh-authentication rule unimplementable, because there is nothing left to authenticate freshly against.
**A3 — A signed self-contained cookie with no session table.** *Rejected*; §3.2. Revocation degenerates to a denylist, which is a session table with worse properties.
**A4 — A shared shop PIN or a shared device login.** *Rejected*, and the bead itself refuses it in four words: **"No global pilot password."** It also destroys T35(c) reconciliation and 022 P1's blame clause in the same stroke — a defect traced to "the counter phone" is a defect traced to nobody, which sounds protective and means the employee cannot show it was not them.
**A5 — Passkeys now, TOTP never.** *Rejected*; §4.2, on the recovery ground rather than the security one.
**A6 — A synchronizer CSRF token.** *Rejected*; §5.2. A fourth mechanism whose failure is silent, behind three whose failures are loud.
**A7 — A mutable `app_session.last_seen_at` for idle expiry.** *Rejected*; §3.3. It is a write per request on a row the whole security model reads, and it reintroduces the mutable-state shape 040, 042 and 047 each refused. Rotation gives the same property over immutable rows and yields reuse detection as a by-product.
**A8 — Per-IP rate limiting or lockout at the authentication boundary.** *Rejected*; §9.4 and 042 §8.1, which this record does not reopen.

### 12.2 Consequences

**What gets better.**
- **Every gap 046 ranked below G-1 becomes expressible.** G-2 (the shop picker), G-3 (RLS needs a tenant to set), G-10 (a credential write surface needs an owner), G-11 (the G2 defect assertion) and G-20 (the rate key) each named authentication as their precondition; four of the five are unblocked by §6 alone and one is *closed* by §6.2.
- **The accessor module gets something to gate** (E8, 046 E23), and `auth_attempt` joins it at birth rather than being added to it later.
- **Five migrations' worth of comments stop being promissory** (E6): `operator_id` acquires a writer and an FK target.

**What gets worse, stated plainly.**
- **This is the largest single subsystem in E03, and it is on the critical path of five others.** §10.2's split is a mitigation, not a reduction.
- **A session read joins the hot path of every request, and it is a LOCK, not just a read** (R3, K1, K3, answering Q6). It is one indexed lookup inside a transaction that already exists (§0) — taking `FOR NO KEY UPDATE`, at a fixed position in 042 §5.3(b)'s lock order, on the one row every request must read. **Two things follow and neither is free.** A membership write blocks behind that person's in-flight requests and vice versa (§3.4), which is rare and correct. And **the session row joins every mutating transaction's write set on rotation**, which is Q2's real content: it makes the security-critical row a **serialization-failure source** under the `40001` retry 041 §4.5 already budgets. **That is why rotation and not `last_seen_at`**: a mutable `last_seen_at` puts a write on that row in **every** transaction rather than one in every rotation period, so the same failure mode arrives at the request rate instead of the rotation rate. §13.0's Q2 ruling states it as the answer rather than leaving it in a consequences list.
- **Rotation writes rows.** The volume is bounded by a period nobody has measured yet (§3.3), and the first honest number arrives from a real batch.
- **A PIN is a low-entropy secret, and the argument for it is a chain of three controls** (§3.5). If any one of them is implemented weakly — a lockout in memory, a PIN accepted without a device session, an enrollment code that does not expire — the whole argument fails quietly. **I10 exists because that failure is quiet.**
- **The operator picker renders names, and one sort order away from it is a leaderboard** (§3.5, I7). This record makes a P3 boundary depend on a property of a payload.
- **Operator attribution is not non-repudiable, and this record creates the surface that will be mistaken for proof** (R7, the security lens's D2). A coworker who watches a PIN can act as that person on that phone, and the system will record it as theirs. **The mitigation is a linguistic rule, not a control** — §3.5's RULE, enforced at 021's T26 pre-send — and a rule about what may be said is the weakest kind of mitigation there is. It is stated as a thing that got worse rather than as a thing that was handled.
- **One engineer now maintains an authentication system** (§12.1 A1). Not a subsystem that ships; a subsystem that has to keep being right.
- **Nothing here is built.** Every invariant in §11 names a test that does not exist, and §2–§9 sit behind a migration 034 specified and nobody has written.

### 13.1 The nine questions, as put

Nine, and the first four are the ones a lens should be able to move.

1. **Should this have been bought rather than built (§12.1 A1)?** The four grounds are the shared-phone construction, the processor cap, the local FK, and the availability dependency. **Is the shared-counter-phone shape genuinely unbuyable, or is it a device session plus a hosted user store, in which case the build shrinks to §3.5 and §6?**
2. **Is §3.3's rotation-instead-of-touch the right way to get idle expiry over immutable rows, or is it a write-amplification trap wearing a Hickey costume?** The claim is that rotations are rare relative to requests. **Nobody has measured that, and §0 forbids quoting a number for it.** Is a session table that is a *declared config exemption* with a mutable `last_seen_at` — the shape `shop_credentials` and `request_idempotency` already have — the more honest answer, given that both of those are exemptions precisely because they are statements about the present?
3. **Is a PIN on a device session sufficient authentication for an act that a shop's whole catalog depends on?** The chain is three controls (§3.5) and it fails quietly if any is weak. **Is there a construction with the same ergonomics and a shorter chain?**
4. **Is §5's refusal to issue a CSRF token correct, or is it three controls that are each owned by another record and can each be changed by a bead that has never read this one?** Mechanism (2) is 042's idempotency header; mechanism (3) is the absence of a dependency. **I6 asserts all three — is an asserted absence a control?**
5. **`SHOP_NOT_FOUND` for a wrong tenant (§6.5): correct, or an operability cost this pilot cannot pay?** The oracle argument is real and so is the support call. **Does 019 T24's "cross-tenant access = 0" reach the fact of existence, or only the rows?**
6. **Does §3.4's rotate-on-privilege-change hold under concurrency?** A membership write and a request on the affected session commit in different transactions on different connections. **Is "rotate every live session in the same transaction as the membership write" actually sufficient, or does it need the session read to take a lock, and if so does that put a lock on the hot path of every request?**
7. **Is §4.2's TOTP ruling right, or is it a decision made against a recovery problem that §8's recovery codes already solve?** If recovery codes are the primary path for a lost factor, the fourth ground — the decisive one — weakens considerably. **Does the passkey case survive its own recovery story?**
8. **Is §7.2's out-of-band invitation an honest v0 or a deferred subsystem?** It is stronger for a three-person shop and it does not obviously scale to 038's national case. **At what shop size does "the owner reads a code across the counter" stop being the right answer, and should that threshold be written here or discovered at E13?**
9. **Is §9.1's `auth_attempt` a T35 hazard rather than a T35 control?** It is per-operator data, created to defend the system, placed behind the break-glass accessor. **022 P3's CFO constraint is that no per-operator surface may be built and then restricted. Is an append-only failure log a surface, or is it the audit substrate T35(c) explicitly requires?**

### Recommended lenses

**`security-auditor` and `martin-kleppmann-reviewer`.**

**`security-auditor`** needs no defence: it is the lens that will attack the PIN chain (Q3), argue with the CSRF refusal (Q4), and is the only available lens whose default posture is adversarial. It ran on 046 and its five dissents there — above all D5, that the aggregate instrument is *"a mitigation and not an equivalent"* — are the reasoning discipline this record needs applied to §3.5 and §9.

**`martin-kleppmann-reviewer` is the second, and it is chosen over `rich-hickey-reviewer` deliberately, although the Hickey case is genuinely strong here.** The Hickey case is Q2: this record's spine is *a session is a series of facts, not a mutable row*, which is his separation applied to authority, and §3.3's rotation construction is exactly the move he would either bless or call a cache with delusions. **The reason to take Kleppmann anyway is that the questions with teeth are about time and concurrency, not shape.** Q6 is a two-transaction race on privilege change; Q2's real content is write amplification against an unmeasured rate; §3.3's reuse detection is an at-most-once-supersession argument of exactly the form he ruled on in 047 A1 and A10; and §3.4's *"in the same transaction"* is a claim about a boundary he has twice found stated more strongly than the mechanism supported (042 A8, 043 A3). **A session is the one object in this system where being briefly wrong about the present is a security outcome**, and that is his axis.

**`rich-hickey-reviewer` is the named substitution and the case for it is Q2 and Q9** — both are "is this a fact or is this state" questions. If the acting head wants pressure on whether §3.3 has re-invented a status column with extra steps, rather than on whether it is correct under concurrency, Hickey is the swap to make. **Running all three is defensible on a record this size and is not recommended**: 047's experience was that a third lens offered for one question re-litigates a settled record, and Q2 is a question either of these two can answer.
